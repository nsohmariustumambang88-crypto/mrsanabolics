The World Anti-Doping Agency (WADA) publishes the list of substances and methods that are banned in sport, and every organisation that has signed the World Anti-Doping Code has to apply it. This article explains the 2026 list: what its anabolic agents section covers, how the list changes each year, how medical exemptions work, and the sanctions, including what changes on 1 January 2027, when a new list and a revised Code take effect.

It names the exact instruments, which change every autumn. It says nothing about detection windows or anything else that would help someone avoid a positive test.

> **At a glance**
> - The 2026 Prohibited List is in force until the 2027 List takes effect on 1 January 2027.[^1][^2]
> - Anabolic agents are section S1, prohibited at all times, in and out of competition. S1 reads the same in the 2027 List.[^1][^3]
> - Under the Code in force in 2026, a first violation involving an anabolic steroid carries a four-year ban unless the athlete establishes it was not intentional.[^4]
> - A revised World Anti-Doping Code, with a revised sanctions scheme, takes effect on 1 January 2027.[^5]

## What is the WADA Prohibited List?

The Prohibited List is "a mandatory International Standard as part of the World Anti-Doping Program".[^1] It is mandatory for every signatory of the World Anti-Doping Code,[^2] which was first adopted in 2003 and took effect in 2004.[^5]

The rule underneath it is strict liability. Article 2.1.1 of the Code says: "It is the Athletes' personal duty to ensure that no Prohibited Substance enters their bodies." Athletes are responsible for any prohibited substance found in their sample, and "it is not necessary that intent, Fault, Negligence or knowing Use on the Athlete's part be demonstrated" to establish a violation.[^4] Possession is a violation in its own right: possessing a substance that is prohibited out of competition, such as an anabolic steroid, breaks the rules unless the athlete shows it is consistent with a Therapeutic Use Exemption or has another acceptable justification.[^4]

## Prohibited at all times vs in competition

The List divides into three groups:[^1]

| Group | Sections | What they cover |
|---|---|---|
| Prohibited at all times (in and out of competition) | S0 to S5, and methods M1 to M3 | Non-approved substances, anabolic agents, peptide hormones and growth factors, beta-2 agonists, hormone and metabolic modulators, diuretics and masking agents, and prohibited methods |
| Prohibited in competition | S6 to S9 | Stimulants, narcotics, cannabinoids, glucocorticoids |
| Prohibited in particular sports | P1 | Beta-blockers |

The Code says the substances banned at all times are those prohibited "because of their potential to enhance performance in future Competitions or their masking potential".[^4] For anabolic agents the distinction does not arise: they are banned at all times.[^1]

## What S1 Anabolic Agents covers

Every substance in S1 is prohibited at all times and is a "non-Specified Substance", and the section opens simply: "Anabolic agents are prohibited."[^1] It has two parts.

### S1.1 Anabolic androgenic steroids

S1.1 prohibits anabolic androgenic steroids "when administered exogenously", that is, from outside the body. It lists dozens of examples, "including but not limited to":[^1]

- testosterone, nandrolone, trenbolone, boldenone and drostanolone
- stanozolol, oxandrolone, oxymetholone, metandienone and methyltestosterone
- metenolone, mesterolone, fluoxymesterone and trestolone (MENT)
- related compounds such as androstenedione and prasterone (DHEA)
- tetrahydrogestrinone (THG) and desoxymethyltestosterone

The section ends with "and other substances with a similar chemical structure or similar biological effect(s) including their esters".[^1] A steroid does not have to be named to be prohibited, and neither does any ester of one.

### S1.2 Other anabolic agents

S1.2 names clenbuterol, osilodrostat, ractopamine, zeranol, zilpaterol and the selective androgen receptor modulators (SARMs), giving andarine, enobosarm (ostarine), LGD-4033 (ligandrol), RAD140, S-23 and YK-11 as examples.[^1] For how SARMs are treated outside sport, see [are SARMs legal?](/blog/are-sarms-legal-fda-wada-tga)

### Related sections

Several substances often mentioned alongside steroids sit in other sections, which are also prohibited at all times:[^1]

- **S2** covers testosterone-stimulating peptides in males, including chorionic gonadotrophin (CG) and luteinising hormone (LH), as well as growth hormone and its releasing factors.
- **S4** covers aromatase inhibitors such as anastrozole, exemestane and letrozole, and anti-oestrogenic substances such as clomifene, tamoxifen and raloxifene.
- **S0** catches any pharmacological substance not addressed elsewhere on the List and not approved by any government health authority for human therapeutic use, such as drugs approved only for veterinary use. BPC-157 is named as an example.

### Specified and non-specified substances

Under the Code, every prohibited substance is a "Specified Substance" unless the List says otherwise. As the List explains, that does not make them less dangerous: they are simply substances "more likely to have been consumed or used by an Athlete for a purpose other than the enhancement of sport performance".[^1] Every substance in S1 is non-specified, which matters for the sanctions below. The aromatase inhibitors and anti-oestrogens in S4.1 and S4.2 are specified.[^1]

## How the list is updated each year

The Code requires WADA to publish the List "as often as necessary and no less often than annually". A new List comes into effect three months after WADA publishes it, unless it says otherwise.[^4]

WADA's yearly cycle starts with a meeting in January. Its List Expert Advisory Group gathers scientific and medical research, trends, and intelligence from law enforcement and pharmaceutical companies. A draft is circulated to stakeholders and revised in light of their submissions, WADA's Health, Medical and Research Committee makes recommendations, and the Executive Committee approves the List at its September meeting. The List is published by 1 October.[^2]

To be added, a substance or method must meet at least two of three criteria: it has the potential to enhance or enhances sport performance; it represents an actual or potential health risk to athletes; it violates the spirit of sport.[^2] A substance or method is also included if there is scientific evidence that it can mask the use of others. WADA's decisions on what goes on the List, and in which category, are final, and an athlete cannot challenge them by arguing that a substance does not enhance performance or pose a health risk.[^5]

### What changes for 2027

WADA's Executive Committee approved the 2027 List on 10 September 2026, WADA published it on 21 September 2026, and it enters into force on 1 January 2027.[^2] The major modifications WADA lists concern cannabinoids (S8), glucocorticoids (S9, where vaginal administration becomes a permitted route), beta-blockers (P1), the wording on substances of abuse and the title of S4.2, plus added examples in four classes. GLP-1 receptor agonists such as semaglutide and tirzepatide stay unchanged pending more data.[^2] S1 is not among the changes, and its text is the same in the 2026 and 2027 Lists.[^1][^3]

## Therapeutic Use Exemptions (TUEs)

The List notes that some S1 substances may be found in medications used to treat male hypogonadism.[^1] The rules for using a prohibited substance as treatment are in the International Standard for Therapeutic Use Exemptions (ISTUE). The 2023 edition applies until the end of 2026; a revised edition, approved by WADA's Executive Committee on 5 December 2025, takes effect on 1 January 2027.[^6][^7]

Under both, an athlete who needs a prohibited substance for therapeutic reasons must apply for and obtain a TUE before using or possessing it, unless they qualify to apply retroactively, for example after emergency or urgent treatment.[^6][^7] A TUE may be granted only if the athlete shows, on the balance of probabilities, that each of four criteria is met. In the 2027 wording:[^7]

1. The athlete has a diagnosed medical condition supported by relevant clinical evidence.
2. The substance is an indicated treatment for the condition, and there is no reasonable permitted therapeutic alternative.
3. Its therapeutic use will not produce any additional enhancement of performance beyond what might be anticipated by a return to the athlete's normal state of health.
4. The need for it is not a consequence, wholly or in part, of earlier use of a prohibited substance or method without a TUE.

The 2023 edition sets the same four conditions in a different order.[^6] The fourth means that a medical need caused by earlier use of a prohibited substance without a TUE does not qualify.

The 2027 standard adds that the decision rests solely on these criteria, and "does not consider whether the Prohibited Substance or Prohibited Method is the most clinically appropriate or safe, or whether its Use is legal in all jurisdictions".[^7] A TUE is a sporting permission, not medical advice or a legal licence.

## Sanctions under the World Anti-Doping Code

In an individual sport, a violation connected with an in-competition test "automatically leads to Disqualification of the result obtained in that Competition with all resulting Consequences, including forfeiture of any medals, points and prizes".[^4] On top of that comes ineligibility: being barred "for a specified period of time from participating in any Competition or other activity or funding".[^4]

### Until 31 December 2026: the 2021 Code

For the presence, use, attempted use or possession of a non-specified substance, such as an anabolic steroid, Article 10.2 sets four years of ineligibility unless the athlete can establish that the violation was not intentional, in which case it is two years.[^4] "Intentional" covers an athlete who knew the conduct was a violation, or who knew there was a significant risk that it might be and "manifestly disregarded that risk".[^4] These periods are subject to potential elimination, reduction or suspension under Articles 10.5, 10.6 and 10.7.[^4]

### From 1 January 2027: the 2027 Code

The revised Code keeps four years as the starting point for the presence or use of a non-specified substance, and sets out more steps below it:[^5]

| Situation | Period of ineligibility |
|---|---|
| Default | 4 years |
| The athlete shows how the substance entered their system and that the context was unrelated to sport performance, but not that it was unintentional | 3 years |
| The athlete shows how the substance entered their system and that the violation was not intentional | 2 years, which can be reduced further under Articles 10.5, 10.6 or 10.7.1 |
| The athlete cannot show how it entered their system, but in exceptional cases shows with reliable scientific evidence that the violation was not compatible with intentional use | May be reduced to 2 years |

The new Code applies in full from 1 January 2027. A case pending on that date, or brought later about a violation that happened before it, is governed by the substantive rules in force when the violation occurred, unless the hearing panel decides that the principle of "lex mitior" (applying the more lenient rule) should apply.[^5]

## The Athlete Biological Passport

Not every case starts with a substance found in a sample. WADA's Athlete Biological Passport (ABP) "monitors selected biological variables over time that indirectly reveal the effects of doping", complementing tests that look for a substance directly. It builds reference ranges for each athlete, rather than for the population, and a result outside the athlete's own range triggers an alert known as an "atypical passport".[^8]

The steroidal module, launched in 2014, profiles an athlete's steroid variables in urine over time to uncover steroid doping such as the use of testosterone or related compounds; markers measured in blood serum were added in 2023. An atypical passport can lead to further analysis, target testing and investigation, and can support a case for use of a prohibited substance under Article 2.2 of the Code.[^8]

## Checking a medicine

Athletes can check whether a specific medicine is prohibited on Global DRO, run by USADA, UK Anti-Doping, Sport Integrity Canada and Swiss Sport Integrity. It covers products sold in Australia, Canada, Japan, New Zealand, Switzerland, the UK and the US, and it "does not contain information on, or that applies to, any dietary supplements".[^9] For what anti-doping agencies have said about supplements containing undeclared SARMs, see [are SARMs legal?](/blog/are-sarms-legal-fda-wada-tga)
