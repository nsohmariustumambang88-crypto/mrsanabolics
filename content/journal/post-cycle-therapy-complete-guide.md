This article sets out what the research says about post-cycle therapy, or PCT: how steroids switch off the body's own testosterone and sperm production, how well that recovers after stopping, and what the medicines linked with PCT are licensed for. It contains no doses, schedules or recommendations. Those are questions for a doctor who knows your history. If you are new to the subject, start with [what anabolic steroids are](/blog/what-are-anabolic-steroids).

## What is post-cycle therapy (PCT)?

PCT is not a medical treatment. It is a term from steroid-using communities for medicines taken after a course of steroids in the hope of restarting natural hormone production. A 2020 review in *Endocrine Connections* describes it as "an unproven strategy that aims to restore endogenous testosterone production as soon as possible after a cycle of AAS".[^1] AAS stands for anabolic-androgenic steroids.

The Endocrine Society's scientific statement on performance-enhancing drugs gives the typical example: "self-administration of clomiphene and/or human chorionic gonadotropin at the end of a cycle of AAS use".[^2]

## How steroids suppress natural testosterone

In men, testosterone production is controlled by a chain of signals from the brain to the testes, called the hypothalamic-pituitary-gonadal (HPG) axis. The pituitary gland releases luteinising hormone (LH) and follicle-stimulating hormone (FSH). LH stimulates the Leydig cells of the testes to make testosterone.[^3] When LH and FSH are suppressed, sperm production can be suppressed too, and the result can be infertility.[^2]

The axis runs on feedback, so androgens taken from outside the body turn it down: steroid use "suppresses secretion of gonadotropins from the pituitary".[^3] The 2020 review calls this "an inevitable side effect of AAS abuse". Gonadotropin production falls, "mostly to undetectable levels", and the testes stop making testosterone.[^1] Oestrogens also strongly suppress gonadotrophin production.[^1]

The problem shows when the steroids stop. The Endocrine Society notes that users "may become hypogonadal upon discontinuation of AAS use", left without enough testosterone of their own.[^2] The signs include fatigue, loss of libido, erectile dysfunction and depressed mood.[^1]

## How long does it take to recover after steroids?

Four studies are cited most often. Their designs differ, and so do their results. They describe groups of men, not what will happen to any one person.

### The HAARLEM study

The Dutch HAARLEM study followed 100 male amateur athletes forward in time. It tested them before a steroid cycle, at its end, three months after it, and one year after it began.[^4] In most men, testosterone concentrations were back to normal within three months of the end of the cycle. Sperm production took longer, around a year. At the end of follow-up, 9 men (11%) still had testosterone below normal and 25 (34%) still had a total sperm count below 40 million.[^4] The authors conclude that recovery happens "in the vast majority of users", but not in all, and that this may especially be so for athletes "with a high past exposure to androgens". They note that the follow-up was relatively short, given how slowly sperm production recovers.[^4]

### Shankara-Narayana and colleagues, 2020

A cross-sectional study in the *Journal of Clinical Endocrinology & Metabolism* recruited 41 current users, 31 past users who had stopped at least three months earlier, and 21 non-users through social media.[^5] Past users did not differ from non-users, apart from a residual reduction in testicular volume. Mean time to recovery was 7.3 months for anti-Müllerian hormone (AMH), 10.7 months for LH and 14.1 months for sperm output, and FSH and inhibin B took longer. The longer men had used androgens, the slower their sperm output recovered.[^5] The authors called the suppression "effectively fully reversible", apart from testis volume and sex hormone binding globulin, with recovery taking "between 6 to 18 months after ceasing androgen intake".[^5]

### Rasmussen and colleagues, 2016

A case-control study in *PLoS One* compared 33 former users, 37 current users and 30 controls, all recreational strength trainers aged 18 to 50. The former users had stopped at least three months before enrolling, and on average 2.5 years before.[^6] Of the former users, 27.2% had total testosterone below the lower reference limit of 12.1 nmol/l, against none of the controls. Depressive symptoms (24.2%), erectile dysfunction (27.3%) and decreased libido (40.1%) were all more common among former users.[^6]

### Kanayama and colleagues, 2015

A study in *Addiction* compared 24 former long-term users with 36 weightlifters who had never used steroids.[^7] Of the 19 former users not on testosterone treatment, five had testosterone below 200 ng/dl despite 3 to 26 months without steroids. Seven of the 24 (29%) had a major depressive episode during withdrawal.[^7] The authors concluded that withdrawal hypogonadism "appears to be common, frequently prolonged and associated with substantial morbidity".[^7]

The Endocrine Society's reading of the wider evidence is that after a lengthy course the axis "may be suppressed for months, or years; and some individuals may never regain normal testosterone levels". Steroids may also harm the testes directly, so some men stay hypogonadal even after the signals from the brain recover.[^2]

## Do you need PCT? What the evidence says

No study has shown that it works.

- The Endocrine Society: "To date, we are not aware of any systematic treatment studies in AAS-induced hypogonadism." Case reports describe both success and failure with clomiphene and hCG.[^2]
- A 2014 review in *Fertility and Sterility* was "Intended as a meta-analysis, but no quality studies met the inclusion criteria."[^8]
- A 2019 review in the *Journal of Clinical Endocrinology & Metabolism* states that "The evidence for effective, safe management of AAS cessation and withdrawal is weak", and that "there are no clinical trials of medical therapy for AAS withdrawal."[^3]

The nearest thing to a test comes from HAARLEM. Eighty of its 100 men used medicines they called PCT, most often tamoxifen, hCG or clomiphene. "PCT was not associated with total or free testosterone, or TSC" (total sperm count) at either later visit. The authors wrote: "We were unable to demonstrate any beneficial effect of medications referred to as PCT, therefore the use of these agents should be discouraged."[^4] The men chose for themselves, so the study cannot rule out a benefit in some group. It found no sign of one.

Doctors do sometimes use these medicines. The *Fertility and Sterility* review names judicious testosterone replacement, hCG and selective estrogen receptor modulators among the management options.[^8] The 2019 review concludes that men left infertile by long-term, heavy use might benefit from short-term treatment with clomiphene or hCG.[^3] Those are decisions a doctor makes after testing, for a diagnosed problem.

## Clomid, Nolvadex, hCG and anastrozole: what they are licensed for

These are the medicines most often linked with PCT. Each is a prescription medicine with an FDA prescription label, and none of those labels lists recovery after steroid use as an indication.[^9][^10][^11][^12] As the 2019 review puts it, the US Food and Drug Administration "has not approved the use of clomiphene, hCG, or testosterone for the treatment of AAS withdrawal".[^3]

Aromatase inhibitors and selective estrogen receptor modulators such as clomiphene "increase circulating LH that stimulates Leydig cells to synthesize testosterone".[^3] But the 2020 review says their use as PCT rests on "the unproven assumption that they will speed up recovery of the male hypothalamic-pituitary-gonadal axis".[^1]

### Clomiphene (Clomid)

Clomiphene's US label licenses it "for the treatment of ovulatory dysfunction in women desiring pregnancy". The label states: "There are no adequate or well-controlled studies that demonstrate the effectiveness of clomiphene citrate in the treatment of male infertility." It adds that "testicular tumors and gynecomastia have been reported in males using clomiphene".[^9]

Its warnings include blurred vision and other visual symptoms, which "may be irreversible, especially with increased dosage or duration of therapy".[^9] The 2019 review warns that clomiphene might increase the risk of venous thrombosis, and notes case reports of it in men.[^3]

### Tamoxifen (Nolvadex)

Tamoxifen is licensed for breast cancer, including metastatic breast cancer "in women and men", and for reducing the risk in women at high risk.[^10] Its boxed warning, for women with ductal carcinoma in situ or at high risk, says that serious and life-threatening events "include uterine malignancies, stroke and pulmonary embolism". The label also warns of deep vein thrombosis and cataracts. In men treated for breast cancer, "Loss of libido and impotence have resulted in discontinuation of tamoxifen therapy".[^10]

### hCG (human chorionic gonadotropin)

hCG works differently. Its label says: "The action of HCG is virtually identical to that of pituitary LH."[^11] It is licensed for undescended testes in boys, for "Selected cases of hypogonadotropic hypogonadism (hypogonadism secondary to a pituitary deficiency) in males", and for inducing ovulation. Listed adverse reactions include headache, irritability, restlessness, depression, fatigue, oedema, gynaecomastia and pain at the injection site.[^11]

Because hCG does not stimulate the pituitary, the 2020 review warns that "It may actually delay recovery of gonadotrophin production by artificially increasing plasma testosterone levels and thereby prolonging the underlying hypothalamic suppression of reproductive function."[^1]

### Aromatase inhibitors (anastrozole, Arimidex)

Anastrozole is licensed only for breast cancer in postmenopausal women.[^12] Its label warns of ischaemic cardiovascular events in women with pre-existing ischaemic heart disease, of reduced bone mineral density, and of raised cholesterol.[^12] The 2019 review notes that many men take an aromatase inhibitor alongside steroids to try to prevent breast growth, and that this has not been shown to be effective.[^3]

## What a doctor can do

The most useful step is to tell a doctor what you have taken, because tests show where recovery stands. The *Fertility and Sterility* review calls for a complete endocrine and metabolic assessment.[^8] The 2019 review describes confirming the diagnosis with "an early morning serum testosterone and gonadotropins" and, for men concerned about fertility, semen analysis for "sperm concentration, motility, and morphology".[^3]

Low LH, FSH and testosterone together are typical of the recovery phase. Raised gonadotrophins, especially FSH out of proportion to LH, should instead make a doctor suspect "primary gonadal dysfunction, unrelated to steroid abuse".[^1] In the experience of the 2020 review's authors, sperm counts return to normal later than testosterone, so a normal testosterone result does not settle the fertility question.[^1] The same review advises that testosterone replacement "should be withheld as long as possible, since it interferes with HPG axis recovery".[^1]

Stopping can also affect mood. The NHS lists depression and apathy, anxiety, trouble concentrating, insomnia, lower sex drive, extreme tiredness, headaches, and muscle and joint pain among withdrawal symptoms.[^13]

> **If you feel low after stopping**
> Depression during withdrawal is a recognised effect: in one study, 29% of former long-term users had a major depressive episode.[^7] Tell a doctor. If you are having thoughts of suicide, contact emergency services or a crisis line where you live straight away.

## Where to get confidential help

The NHS advises seeing a GP if you think you are addicted to anabolic steroids, and says a GP may refer you to a drugs counsellor.[^13]

- **UK:** your GP, or [FRANK](https://www.talktofrank.com/drug/image-and-performance-enhancing-drug) on 0300 123 6600 or by text to 82111.[^14]
- **United States:** SAMHSA's National Helpline, 1-800-662-4357, which is free, confidential and open 24/7.[^15]
