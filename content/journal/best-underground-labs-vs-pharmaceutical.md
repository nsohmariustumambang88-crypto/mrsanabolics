"Underground lab" and "pharma grade" are two of the most used phrases in the steroid market, and two of the least examined. The difference between them is not a brand, a label design or a price. It is whether anyone licensed, inspected and checked the place a product was made. This article sets out what "pharmaceutical grade" means in law, what an underground lab is, and what published laboratory analyses of black-market steroids have found.

## What "pharmaceutical grade" actually means

Outside marketing, "pharmaceutical grade" means made by a licensed manufacturer under good manufacturing practice (GMP): the rules regulators use to control how medicines are made, tested and released.

### In the United States

The FDA's current good manufacturing practice (CGMP) regulations "contain the minimum current good manufacturing practice" for making a drug, so that it "has the identity and strength and meets the quality and purity characteristics that it purports or is represented to possess". A drug made without complying with them is deemed adulterated.[^1]

Two of the rules matter most for steroids. Each batch must be tested for "the identity and strength of each active ingredient" before it is released. And a product sold as sterile must be made under written procedures "designed to prevent microbiological contamination", which "shall include validation of all aseptic and sterilization processes".[^2]

The FDA is clear that testing a finished product is not enough on its own: "While CGMP requires testing, testing alone is not adequate to ensure quality." Its example is a manufacturer that tests 100 tablets from a batch of 2 million.[^3] That is why the rules govern how the product is made at every step, not just how it is checked at the end, and why "FDA inspects pharmaceutical manufacturing facilities worldwide".[^3]

### In the European Union and the United Kingdom

In the EU, the GMP rules for medicines are collected in EudraLex Volume 4. It includes an annex on the manufacture of sterile medicinal products, fully applicable since 25 August 2024.[^4]

In the UK, "To make, assemble or import human medicines, you need a manufacturer's licence". To get one, a company must show the MHRA that it complies with GMP and "pass regular GMP inspections of your site". Licensed manufacturers can be looked up on the MHRA's GMDP website and on the European Medicines Agency's database.[^5]

So "pharmaceutical grade" is a fact about a manufacturer that can be checked against a register. It is not a phrase on a label.

## What an underground lab is

"Underground lab" is the market's name for a manufacturer with none of the above: no manufacturing licence, no GMP inspection, and no regulator checking batches before they are released. Researchers use the term too. One study included in a 2022 meta-analysis of black-market steroid analyses recorded 49 manufacturers, "the majority of them being underground labs and only the minority being pharmaceutical companies".[^6]

The authors of that meta-analysis note that products from clandestine laboratories do not go through microbiological quality control. They conclude that the substandard and counterfeit products they found "were most likely produced by manufacturers not in line with good manufacturing practices".[^6]

The World Health Organization uses two terms that fit what those studies found. "Substandard products are those that do not meet quality standards and specifications", and "Falsified medical products deliberately misrepresent their identity, composition or source". WHO says substandard products often result from poor manufacturing practices or inadequate quality control.[^7]

## What lab tests of black-market steroids have found

### Content and dose against the label

The largest summary is the 2022 systematic review and meta-analysis by Magnolini and colleagues. It pooled 19 studies from Europe and Brazil, covering 5,413 samples.[^6]

| Finding | Pooled share |
|---|---|
| Counterfeit: the contents did not match the label | 36% |
| Substandard: the right substance, in an amount outside the accepted range | 37% |
| Counterfeits with no active ingredient | 24% of counterfeits |
| Counterfeits with a different active ingredient | 44% of counterfeits |
| Counterfeits missing a labelled ingredient, or with one added | 11% of counterfeits |
| Substandard samples under strength | 67% of substandard |
| Substandard samples over strength | 33% of substandard |

All figures in the table are from the meta-analysis.[^6] "Accepted range" was not one fixed standard. The included studies used ranges as wide as 50–200% and as narrow as 90–110% of the label claim, and the authors write that the proportions "must be interpreted with caution".[^6]

Two later studies add detail.

- **Denmark.** Police in three districts seized 764 products between December 2019 and December 2020, from 193 identifiable manufacturing companies. Anabolic steroids were the most common class, found in 60% of products. In 25–34% of products, the active ingredient was missing or was not the one stated. Only 7–10% contained no active ingredient or a compound from a different class.[^8]
- **Australia.** A forensic laboratory analysed 28 anonymously donated samples. Fifteen were mislabelled or mis-sold. Of the 21 samples with a clearly defined labelled dose, 4 were within a ±5% deviation of the expected purity.[^9]

### Injectables compared with tablets

A 2009 UK study tested 38 injectable and 19 oral samples obtained from the underground market: "Fifty-three per cent (20) of the injectable AAS esters and 21% (4) of the oral tablets were counterfeit."[^10] The 2022 meta-analysis reports counterfeit proportions for oil-based solutions compared with tablets "described as 43–65% vs. 29–37%" in the studies it drew on, and says the proportions for injectables "may be considerably higher".[^6]

### Bacterial contamination of injectables

The 2009 study was prompted by "a series of intramuscular abscesses, requiring surgical treatment" in the UK in 2007. "Culture and sensitivity revealed the presence of skin commensal organisms", which the authors suggest may have contributed to the abscesses.[^10]

A 2025 Dutch study cultured 63 androgen products donated by 31 people in a harm-reduction cohort, bought through the usual black-market channels, mainly webshops and local dealers.[^11]

- 3 of the 63 products (5%) showed bacterial contamination.[^11]
- 2 of 22 used multidose vials (9%) were contaminated, and 1 of 41 unused ampoules and vials (2%).[^11]
- None of the participants developed an injection-related infection linked to a contaminated product.[^11]

The authors say the sample is too small to estimate the true rate, and that repeatedly drawing from a vial may add contamination during use, as well as at manufacture.[^11] Set that against the US rule above: a licensed product sold as sterile must be made under validated sterilisation and aseptic processes.[^2]

### Heavy metals

The Australian study also tested for metals. Twelve heavy metals were detected in injectable and oral products.[^9] The testing was funded by a company that sells testing kits, whose chief executive is one of the authors; the paper declares this.[^9] The authors call for a comprehensive surveillance and testing system.[^9]

We looked for published measurements of particles in illicit injectable steroids and found none, so this article makes no claim about them.

## Why a "pharma grade" label proves nothing

A label is the easiest part of a product to fake. WHO describes falsified products as ones that "deliberately misrepresent their identity, composition or source", made with "sophisticated falsification techniques that are difficult to detect".[^7]

The Danish study shows what that looks like in practice. "Most products had a professional appearance fulfilling most EU requirements for packaging information", yet a quarter to a third did not contain the active ingredient they claimed. In the authors' words, many products "appear professional to the user giving an impression of a high-quality product".[^8]

Three points follow from that:

- **A company name is not a licence.** "Pharma", "Pharmaceuticals" or "Medical" on a label is a word. The test is whether the manufacturer named holds a licence on a regulator's register.[^5]
- **A licensed name can be copied.** Even a manufacturer that is on the register tells you nothing about a pack that did not come from it. Falsified products misrepresent their source.[^7]
- **Genuine EU packs carry safety features.** Prescription medicines in the EU have carried a unique identifier and an anti-tampering device on the outer pack since 9 February 2019.[^12] A pack presented as a licensed EU medicine without them deserves suspicion.

## Underground lab vs pharmaceutical: side by side

| | Licensed manufacturer | Underground lab |
|---|---|---|
| Licence to manufacture | Required[^5] | None |
| GMP inspection | Regular inspections of the site[^3][^5] | None |
| Batch release | Each batch tested for identity and strength before release[^2] | No requirement; you rely on the seller |
| Sterile injectables | Validated sterilisation and aseptic processes required[^2] | No microbiological quality control[^6] |
| What studies of the black market found | Not what these studies measured | 36% counterfeit and 37% substandard, pooled across 19 studies[^6] |

The last row is the honest limit of the evidence. The studies above analysed products from the black market, which is where both underground products and counterfeit "pharma" products are found. None of them compared licensed medicines bought from a pharmacy with underground products.

## What you can check yourself

Nothing a buyer can check turns an unlicensed product into a GMP one. What you can do is narrow the uncertainty.

1. **Look up the manufacturer.** If a label names a pharmaceutical company, check whether it appears on a regulator's register of licensed manufacturers.[^5]
2. **Check the pack.** The batch or lot number and expiry date should be printed and should match on the box, the container and any certificate. [How to spot counterfeit steroids](/blog/how-to-spot-counterfeit-steroids) covers the other packaging checks.
3. **Verify any certificate.** A certificate of analysis describes one tested sample. [How to read a certificate of analysis](/blog/how-to-read-a-certificate-of-analysis) explains how to confirm the laboratory is real, the test is on its accredited scope and the batch is yours.
4. **Know what a test can prove.** A laboratory answers three different questions about a product, and a certificate should say which it answered.
